# The land registry of villages and towns: lots with and without land, and where they lie

*2026-10-16, for #266 (the first map's village) and the city's blocks (D-060). The owner's
question: look at the cadastre of small villages and of small, medium and large towns, medium
density at most, where houses have land attached or not depending on where they stand, and not
every property has an inner court or land; how they are distributed across the area.*

The sources are French planning documents (the CAUE's typology sheets, two urban-form and
density studies, the PLH of Grand Avignon, the national survey of building plots) and the
INSEE. The figures are theirs; the shares marked *estimate* are mine, to be measured on the
cadastre itself (§6). Sizes of places, by the INSEE's urban units: a village or rural commune
under 2 000 people in its built-up area, a small town 2 000–20 000, a medium town
20 000–100 000, a large town over that.

## 1. What a plot is, by where it stands

The same gradient appears in every source, at every size of place: the oldest core is built
solid on narrow plots, the roads out of it carry strips with gardens behind, and the
twentieth century's houses stand free on big lots beyond. The size of the place changes how
many rings there are and how far each reaches, not their order.

**The old core (centre ancien, cœur de village).**
- Plots narrow and deep, the house across the whole width, party walls on both sides, the
  front on the street line. The CAUE of Bouches-du-Rhône on the Provençal village house: a
  street front of **5 to 10 m**, party walls, "plots of reduced size which the houses generally
  occupy in full"; where the front steps back, **1 to 5 m** make a threshold, a terrace or a
  small garden [1]. The CAUE of Val-de-Marne on the bourg house: built to the street line, in
  party, "over the whole width of the plot (**7 to 10 m**), from boundary to boundary" [2].
  Mantes-la-Jolie's inventory: a medieval pattern "narrow and elongated", with several plots
  **under 5 m wide** by the market square [3]. Baume-les-Dames: "the small width of the plots
  on the street implies, in fact, built party walls" [4].
- The depth of the house, not of the plot, is what the street sees: the Chevreuse park's
  guide gives the bourg house one, two or three bays on **5 to 15 m** of front, a wall
  **5 to 8 m** high (a ground floor, one storey, rarely a second, a roof at 35–45°), the
  front "public and visible", the back "private or semi-private and hidden" [5].
- **With or without land, by position:** "depending on the position in the bourg, the plots
  are dense and saturated with buildings, or partly taken by gardens"; behind the house on
  the street, "lower secondary volumes often take the plot; set in party they enclose a
  paved court" serving a more modest dwelling, sheds or service rooms [5]. The bourgeois
  house near the bourg: a gate, a paved court between the house and its annex, the garden
  behind [5]. So the core's plot has, from the street: the house; then nothing (a saturated
  plot), or a court walled by outbuildings, or a garden at the back where the strip is long.
- Density: **80 to 180 dwellings a hectare** for centres anciens in the Grand Avignon's
  six-tissue scale ("continuous built fabric of high density with mixed functions", the
  city centres and the villages alike) [6]. At village scale the figures are lower because
  the houses are low: the heart of a Béarn bastide with its lanière plots, **133 dwellings
  on 5.1 ha, 26 a hectare**, at R+C to R+1+C; a Béarn village along its road, a lanière
  pattern perpendicular to the street, **20 dwellings on 2.0 ha, 10 a hectare** at R+C to
  R+1+C; the cité of Lescar's narrow plots in R+1+C "aligned on the street, opening on private
  outdoor spaces at the back (courts, gardens)", **77 dwellings on 4.1 ha, 19 a hectare** [7].

**The lanière, the plot the whole thing turns on.** In the bastide's heart and "along the
linear faubourgs" the strip plot "allows a tight alignment of the buildings on the street
while freeing space at the back of the plot; the private gardens make a planted weave that
breaks with the mineral public space" [7]. In the Béarn village the lanière is perpendicular to
the road; and the side of the street decides what the strip holds: on the west side the house
steps back behind a court that joins the street, on the east side the house stands on the
street line with its court or garden at the back [7]. A faubourg is this strip pattern pulled
along the roads out of the core.

**The faubourg, the couronne (the ring round the core).**
- Grand Avignon's "couronnes urbaines", the faubourgs built from the end of the 19th century:
  **30 to 50 dwellings a hectare**, "characterised not by their density but by their great
  variety, mixing continuous buildings on the street line and buildings at the back of the
  plots, activities and housing, gaps"; round the intramuros of Avignon and "round the
  Vauclusian village cores" alike [6].
- The PLU's zone UB (the mixed, intermediate fabric) typically takes 40–60 % of the plot
  built, aligned or set back, R+2 to R+4 in towns; zone UA (the old core) 60–80 % built,
  party walls, on the street line [8]. A village's UA is R+1 to R+2.

**The lotissement and the free-standing house (beyond).**
- Grouped individual housing ("individuel dense", houses on small plots without common parts):
  **20 to 30 dwellings a hectare**; lotissements: **10 to 20 a hectare**, a quarter of Grand
  Avignon's built fabric; isolated houses outside any procedure: **5 a hectare**, a fifth of
  the fabric, "the residential development of the agglomeration's villages" [6]. A
  Haute-Savoie study spans the same scale: under 5 a hectare for isolated houses to over 100
  for flats [9].
- The plot of a new detached house in France: **949 m² on average in 2023**, down from 980
  in 2022 and to 932 in 2024; over **1 500 m²** in cheap départements (Nièvre, Yonne), under
  **1 000 m²** in tense ones (Côte-d'Or, Doubs) [10]. In the Pau study's mixed quarter: 40
  free lots "on plots of 1 000 m² on average", beside 22 town houses and 52 grouped
  houses [7]. The PLU's zone UC: setbacks of 3–5 m from the boundaries, 20–40 % built,
  R+1 to R+2 [8].
- Nearly every house built after 1975 has a garden (INSEE, housing conditions 2017) [11];
  individual housing is 56 % of France's dwellings (2019), 39 % of them in rural communes and
  39 % in small urban units [12].

## 2. The distribution across the area

The rings, inside out, with the plot each carries. The shares are my estimates of a village's
houses by ring, to be measured (§6); the sizes are the sources'.

| ring | where | the plot | the house | land attached | share of a village's houses (*estimate*) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| the core | the church, the square, the first streets; 50–150 m across in a village, a few hundred metres in a town | 5–10 m wide, 8–15 m deep where saturated, 15–30 m where a court or garden stays | R+1 (+attic), party walls both sides, on the street line | none on a third to a half of the plots; a walled court or a back garden on the rest | 30–40 % |
| the faubourgs | along the roads out of the core, one plot deep each side | 6–12 m wide, 25–45 m deep (the lanière) | R+1, party or semi-detached, on the line or behind a court | a garden behind, often an outbuilding across the back | 35–45 % |
| the lotissements | the fields taken since the 1960s, off a loop road | 400–1 000 m², 15–25 m wide, 25–40 m deep | one storey or R+1, free on four sides, 5 m back | a garden all round, the house in its middle | 15–30 % |
| the scattered | beyond, along the lanes | over 1 000 m² | free-standing | land all round | a few |

*The generated village at 600 m (2026-10-16, `city::village` on its own plan): the core
block's 30 houses on lots of 68 m², a third with land left (a court of 14 m²); the faubourgs'
89 houses on lots of 135–174 m², nine in ten with a garden of 72–98 m²; the lotissement's 8
houses on 448 m² lots with 363 m² of garden. The core's share without land is at the table's
upper bound, the faubourg's gardens are short of a real lanière's because the blocks are
60 m across and the 30 m strips fell back to 12–22 m: the village's blocks should be cut
longer across for the faubourgs, or its lanes spaced 90 m there.*

In a small town the core and the faubourgs are the same tissues on more streets, with flats over
the shops in the core; a medium town adds the 19th-century ring of closed blocks round courts
(the city's perimeter blocks, D-060) between the core and the faubourgs; a large town's medium
density stops at that ring. The research on three towns' belts describes the same order: an
inner belt "very built", an intermediate one, and an outer one "more recent, more divided, with
larger and greener plots" [13].

What decides whether a plot has land: its ring first (the core's plot is the house, the
faubourg's keeps a garden, the lotissement's is mostly garden), then its depth (a strip of 10 m
behind a 10 m deep house holds nothing, 25 m holds a garden), then its side of the street and
its width (a wide plot in the core holds a court; the Béarn street's west side keeps a court in
front) [5][7].

## 3. What the generator does now, against this

- `city::parcels::strips` cuts every built block into one strip of one depth (14 m for the
  village type) along every street side, and `city::houses` puts a house of the lot's width
  from the street to its plan's depth, a yard of 1.5 m at least behind: every house gets a
  little land, none gets a garden, none stands free. That is the core's pattern applied to the
  whole village, with a yard the core rarely has.
- The city's blocks (D-060) are the ring of perimeter blocks: a building depth per district
  and a court inside. Right for a medium town's ring, not for a core or a faubourg.

## 4. What to do with it (for #266, then the city)

*Point 1 is built (2026-10-16, `city::parcels::ParcelType::{CORE, FAUBOURG, LOTISSEMENT}`
and `city::village`, by distance from the centre; `docs/demos/city-plan.md`, "Its rings").
Points 2 to 4 are open.*

1. **Three lot types by ring**, chosen by a block's distance from the village's centre (the
   square) and by its street (a road out of the core carries faubourg strips):
   - *core*: strips 10–15 m deep, lots 5–10 m wide, the house the whole lot (no yard), a
     court where the lot is wider than 8 m and deeper than 12 m;
   - *faubourg*: strips 25–40 m deep, lots 6–12 m wide, the house at the street line or 4 m
     behind a court (by the sunny side), a garden behind, an outbuilding across the back on
     one lot in three;
   - *lotissement*: lots of 500–1 000 m² (15–25 m by 30–40 m), the house free on four sides
     5 m back from the street, its garden round it; the blocks cut by `ParcelType::width` as
     now but the house set back and narrower than its lot.
2. **The share without land** in the core: a third to a half of the lots keep no court nor
   garden; the rest a court. The rooms-first plan already gives the depth: a household whose
   plan fills the strip has no yard.
3. **Houses from their rooms stay the rule**; the type sets the depth the plan may take and
   whether the house touches the street line.
4. **The city takes the core and faubourg types for its old towns and the streets out of
   them**, block by block, as the research on parcels said (`parcels-and-rooms.md` §5).

## 5. Vocabulary (French, for the rules and the style bible)

*Maison de village* (party walls, the whole plot), *maison de bourg* (the same in a bourg,
a court or garden behind), *maison de ville* (a town's row house, R+1 to R+2), *maison
bourgeoise* (free-standing near the core, a court in front and a garden behind), *pavillon*
(a detached house in a lotissement), *lanière* (the strip plot), *dent creuse* (an unbuilt
gap in a built street), *cœur d'îlot* (the inside of a block), *emprise au sol* (the built
share of the plot), *alignement* (the street line).

## 6. Measuring it for real: the cadastre is open data

The French cadastre is published by Etalab as GeoJSON or Shapefile, layer by layer
(`parcelles`, `batiments`, `communes`, `sections`), by commune, EPCI or département, under
the open licence; since September 2025 every commune is in PCI vecteur [14]. A few megabytes a
commune. With it a small Rust tool (the engine's tests already draw maps) can give, for a
village, a small town, a medium town and a large town's medium-density district, what the
documents only describe:
- the lots' widths and depths along each street, by distance from the mairie;
- the built share of each lot (the `batiments` layer over the `parcelles`), so the share of
  lots without land by ring;
- the courts (an unbuilt part of a lot enclosed by buildings on three sides);
- the lot sizes of the lotissements and how far out they start.

The download waits for the owner's go (the working rule). Candidate communes: a Provençal
village (Meyreuil's core, the CAUE's example), a small town of the Var, a medium town
(Fréjus or Draguignan), and a large town's old ring (Marseille's or Montpellier's faubourgs).
The Cerema's Fichiers fonciers hold the same per lot with the buildings' use, but they are not
open: reserved to entitled bodies [15].
